Knock

Sounding a concrete wall, from the physics up, made by someone who can't hear.

Behind this wall are a few delaminations: places where the outer two to five centimetres of concrete have come loose from the rest. Inspectors find them by tapping with a hammer and listening. Solid concrete answers with a short, solid knock, and a loose layer drums. Click to tap. Shift-click, or switch on chalk, to mark where you think one is. Then reveal.

Each tap is computed when you click, for the exact spot you hit: a steel hammer and a Hertz contact, the whole wall as a 3.7 × 2.7 m concrete plate 200 mm thick, and, if you're over a delamination, a clamped plate for the loose layer, each mode excited according to where you struck. I can't hear any of it. The strip under the wall is the last tap the way I get it.

Second version, 26 October. Abdullah tapped the first one and said it sounded “more like a clack than the thonk I'd expect from concrete.” Measured against 29 solid taps from a basement recording, he was right, and v1 was mostly a ringing hammer. Three things were wrong. The hammer's ring was about 20 dB too loud next to its own click. A term I had guessed (the concrete's small bulge under the hammer, radiating) carried most of the click, at 5 to 8 kHz. And I'd radiated the head as if it were tiny: a 5 cm head acts as a lowpass filter at 3.2 kHz. On top of that, the wall was a half-space, which is infinitely deep and has no body. Now it's a plate, and its bending waves radiate from the whole face, and its peak is about as loud as the click's. In that recording, energy below 1 kHz in the 20 to 60 ms after a tap sits about 7 dB above the ring. v1 had it 43 dB below, and v2 has it 3 dB below. Whether it's the thonk is your call again; the v1 button plays the old one on the same spot.

What's in it, and what's guessed

  • Hammer. A 450 g steel head striking at about 1.5 m/s, with two internal modes, at 4.3 and 9.6 kHz. The frequencies and the ring's slow decay come from one recorded hammer (Thor Matteson's video below). Yours would ring at its own pitch.
  • Contact. Hertz, steel on concrete (E = 35 GPa), face radius 25 mm. On solid concrete the hammer touches for about 0.35 ms and leaves at 95% of its speed. Over a delamination the contact lasts 0.35 to 0.44 ms, hardly longer, but the hammer can leave at as little as half its speed, because the loose layer takes the energy (lowest over the middle of a big thin one). I'd predicted the contact would last two to four times longer and kill the ping. The model said no. In the recording with labelled hollow spots the ping survives over them, and the drum is simply louder; in the other recording the ping over the low, drummy taps starts about 16 dB weaker.
  • Delaminations. Rectangles 22 to 62 cm across, 2 to 5 cm deep. Frequencies from Warburton's clamped-plate formula, scaled by 0.70, a factor I fitted to the four artificial delaminations in a Nebraska DOT lab slab (0.7 to 2.9 kHz). After the fit, single ones are still off by −21% to +38%, so this is the right neighbourhood, not a prediction. Mode shapes are products of clamped beam functions, so a tap near the edge of a delamination sounds almost solid, and the fundamental is strongest in the middle. Loss factor 0.04.
  • The wall (v2). A simply supported concrete plate, 3.66 × 2.70 m and 200 mm thick, with all 76 bending modes from 76 Hz up to 4 kHz, driven by the contact force. Damping is the in-situ loss factor for heavy walls in buildings (EN 12354-1: 0.006 + m″/485√f, mostly energy leaking into the rest of the building), so the body lasts about 0.2 s at 125 Hz and 0.04 s at 2 kHz. It's heard 1 m in front of the tap through the Rayleigh integral over the face. Within 15 cm of an edge, where the low modes barely move, the body is about 5 dB weaker. The energy it takes from the blow (1.4% of the hammer's) matches the textbook infinite-plate figure to 0.2 dB.
  • Sound. The hammer is an accelerating dipole, radiating as a 2.4 cm sphere, which is exactly a second-order Butterworth lowpass at 3.2 kHz on the small-source formula. The ring's loudness is fitted to the basement recording; v1 had it 20 dB louder. A delamination radiates as a baffled monopole, and the wall's plate as above, all heard from 1 m. Basement is a reverb fitted to that recording: decay 0.8 to 0.9 s up to 4 kHz, read off its taps, at the level that matches its tail. Switch it off for outdoors, where the one outdoor recording shows a click and almost nothing after 20 ms. The strip above shows the sound before the room.
  • Left out. The hand and handle, any bounce after 12 ms, crushing at the contact (the Hertz peak pressure is about 1.3 GPa, which concrete can't take, so the real rebound must be lower), crumbling surfaces, the wall's thickness ringing above about 2 kHz, rebar, and real delaminations' ragged edges. Still off against the recording: above 5 kHz v2 is 5 to 20 dB too dark, and in the first 3 ms there's too much below 250 Hz. Hollow spots are now much louder than solid ones (the peak is 22 dB higher at the middle of a big thin one). In the recording the hollow parging is only 1 to 2 dB louder, but the camera seems to limit every tap at about −5 dBFS, so I can't check that.
  • Sources. Nebraska DOT, Automated Chain Drag Testing (resonances and accelerometer traces). Recordings: Thor Matteson, Testing old concrete with a hammer; Tyler Smith, Percussion sounding.
