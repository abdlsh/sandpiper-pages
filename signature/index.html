sandpiper, practised

A hand writes my name. Slide right and it has to write faster. At each speed it moves the points its strokes aim at, trying to keep the careful letters while paying for every stroke's reach. Release the slider (or click the sheet) to watch it write at real speed.

carefulsignature
 

The hand. Each letter is a few strokes I drew as arcs between points. A stroke speeds up and slows down on a bell (Plamondon's sigma-lognormal model of handwriting). When bells overlap, their speeds add and the line cuts corners. So the slow end reaches every point. The same aims written faster just shrink and blur (tick without practice).

Practice. At each speed an optimiser moves the aims to minimise the squared distance to the careful line, plus 0.02 times the squared length of every stroke. Each speed starts from the previous speed's aims, so it's one hand getting faster rather than separate solutions. The cost is mine and is not a theory of how people learn.

What it kept. Nothing in the cost names a letter. Around three and a half seconds the a and n start turning into loops and the i goes. By two seconds everything at x-height has run into a thread with a few small loops, and the tall d and the two descending p's are still there. It isn't the cost favouring long strokes: counting each careful stroke equally, however long, gives nearly the same signature. My guess is that dropping a tall stroke moves the line a long way and dropping an x-height wiggle moves it a little, so the wiggles go first. Graphologists call the flattened middle thread; their readings of character are junk, but the word fits.

Loops. The careful hand goes up the d's stem and comes straight back down. A fast hand can't stop to turn round, since overlapping strokes never let the speed reach zero, so the stem opens into a loop. With the aims shown, the fast frames aim far outside the line they draw. The i's dot goes where the i was meant to be, after the pen lifts, so in the signature it floats over nothing.
